6.1 Literature review

6.1.1 Factors associated with successful smoking cessation

What factors have been identified in previous studies? For example, nicotine dependence, socioeconomic characteristics, depression, e-cigarette use, utilization of smoking cessation services, and exposure to smoking cessation programs or campaigns.

(Vangeli et al. 2011)

  • A systematic review of 8 articles: 4 national surveys (UK, Japan, China), 3 multi-country papers (United States, United Kingdom, Canada, France and Spain, Malaysia and Thailand) ( Canada, the United States, United Kingdom and Australia)

(Hock et al. 2023)

  • Undertook a systematic review of studies that used a statistical model to explore what covariates are associated with quitting success

  • Searched for studies statistically assessing quit attempts in three contexts:

      1. the general population instigated in any setting within the UK
      1. people with a mental health condition instigated in any setting and in any country
      1. initiated within a secondary acute or mental health service in any country.

  • 29 studies were included in the synthesis (21 studies relating to the UK general population, six studies relating to mental health in the UK or Canada, 2 studies relating to secondary care in the UK or Canada)

(Kundu et al. 2024)

Included 11 studies which summarized factors possibly influencing smoking cessation related behaviors including intention to quit smoking, quit attempts and smoking abstinence among young people between age 10 and 35 years

  • young people aged ~10–29 years and who had lower level of nicotine dependence and cravings, had a higher chance of quitting smoking

  • high frequency and intensity of smoking act as barriers for smoking abstinence/quit attempts

    => ‘cutting down’ on smoking or gradual cessation approach targets lowering level of nicotine dependence and increasing probability of quitting

  • stress and depression act as barriers of quitting

  • integrating smoking cessation with the treatment of mental health conditions might be a beneficial intervention approach for young people

  • not using alcohol or other substances were seen to increase probability of smoking abstinence/quit attempts

  • higher self-confidence in quitting, good self-management skills, and being less susceptible to smoking increased probabilities of smoking abstinence/quit attempts among ~10–29 years age young people

  • peer smoking or family or household smoking also may act as barriers for smoking abstinence/quit attempts

    => suggests that group therapy-based smoking cessation programs might work better than individual interventions

  • tobacco price increase, restricting cigarette availability, and ban on cigarette coupons

  • attaining higher education was found to promote smoking abstinence/quit attempts

  • being married/living with partners, pregnancy/becoming parent were positive ‘probable’ predictors, ‘living with children’ was negative ‘possible’ predictors

6.1.2 Socioeconomic and geographic disparities

6.1.2.1 Socieconomic inequalities

Is there evidence of disparities in smoking cessation success across socioeconomic groups or geographic areas?

(Avila et al. 2022)

The Health and Retirement Study (HRS) is a nationally representative longitudinal, biennial survey of individuals 50 and older in the U.S. and their spouses

Aim: assess how socioeconomic status (SES) over the life course impacts smoking cessation among older adult smokers in the U.S

The main exposure variable was both childhood and adulthood SES

  • The childhood SES factor: level of maternal and paternal education, father’s occupation, being born in southern U.S. states, and if the subject grew up in a rural area

  • The adulthood SES variables: respondent’s educational attainment, occupation of longest job, labor force status, household income and household wealth

=> operationalized childhood and adulthood SES as latent factors using full-information maximum likelihood confirmatory factor analysis (CFA)

Life course SES was operationalized into 4 categories to reflect social mobility:

  • persistent low SES (low child SES, low adult SES)

  • low child SES, high adult SES (i.e., upward mobility)

  • high child SES, low adult SES (i.e., downward mobility)

  • persistent high SES (high child SES, high adult SES)

Fixed covariates: birth cohort, sex, race/ethnicity

Time-varying covariates: age, marital status, type of health insurance, doctor visit in the past 12 months, number of cigarettes smoked per day and comorbidities. Death as reason for loss to follow-up (yes vs no) and number of waves completed were also included as time-varying covariates.

Statistical model

  • An unweighted multilevel mixed-effects logistic regression model with a random intercept was estimated to test the effect of life course SES on smoking cessation over time

  • The model was adjusted for life course SES, age centered at 65 years old, the interaction between life course SES and age, sex, race/ethnicity, marital status, health insurance, visit to the doctor in the past 12 months, number of cigarettes smoked per day, comorbidities, number of waves, death, and birth cohort

Findings

  • Those with persistent low SES, upward and downward SES mobility were less likely to quit than those with persistent high SES. However, this association was only significant over time for those with persistent low SES or downward mobility compared to those with persistent high SES

  • low adult SES may continue to impact smoking cessation over time as lower income, education, wealth, occupation and employment status in adulthood are associated with reduced opportunities for access to tobacco cessation treatment and advice to quit, and poorer health literacy

  • lower probability to quit at age 65 for those with persistent low SES, upward and downward SES mobility compared to those with persistent high SES

  • individuals who had upward social mobility from low child to high adult SES were still significantly less likely to quit than those with persistent high SES

  • majority of the older smoker population had persistent high SES, followed by downward mobility, persistent low, and upward mobility

  • older adults of more recent birth cohorts are more likely to quit smoking

=> it may also be important to develop interventions that target these disparities to help ‘heal the scar’ of childhood poverty on late-life health

=> After identifying potential mechanisms underlying the observed disparities, researchers may seek to examine whether extant or novel smoking cessation interventions (e.g., nicotine replacement therapies, Varenicline, contingency management, and other behavioral approaches) demonstrate particular effectiveness in increasing smoking cessation for individuals with low childhood SES.

(Bosdriesz et al. 2015)

  • Aim

Provide an overview of long-term trends of socioeconomic inequalities in smoking cessation in Europe. Data of 11 EU member states from 12 Eurobarometer surveys taken at irregular intervals from 1987 to 2012

  • Variables

Main outcome variable, the quit ratio, was composed of current and former smokers

Two measures of SES: education and occupation

  • ( Education was measured as the age when finished or left full-time education (low≤15 years, middle=16–19 years, high≥20 years))

  • Occupational class was combined into four groups: manual (farmers, fishermen, manual workers), non-manual (office employees, white collar workers, professionals, management), self-employed (business owners, craftsmen) and other/not gainfully employed (students, military service, housewives, unemployed, retired)

  • Individual-level covariates that were included were sex (male/female), and age (continuous when adjusting for confounding, and in four categories when stratifying, 20–34, 35–49, 50–64, 65 years or older).

Statistical model:

  • Used multilevel logistic regression to model associations of the quit ratio (proportion former smokers of ever smokers) with SES, measured by education and occupation separately, with adjustments for age, sex and time.

Findings:

  • From 2000 to 2012 there have been large inequalities in smoking cessation rates in the EU, by education and occupation

Discussion:

  • some evidence that raising taxes might actually have a negative equity impact. An additional effect of tax increases is that smokers, especially from low SES groups, often switch to cheaper brands or cut down rather than quit.

  • Although policies are generally credited with bringing down the overall smoking prevalence, they have not succeeded in decreasing the inequalities in smoking, possibly even increasing them.

  • The finding that socioeconomic inequalities continued to increase during a period of strong improvements in tobacco control policy supports the plea, for future tobacco control policies, to be more specifically targeted at low SES groups.

NoteSummary of smoking cessation success across socioeconomic groups

In US, those with low SES in their life courses were less likely to quit than those with persistent high SES

In EU, there have been large inequalities in smoking cessation rates by education and occupation. Inequalities were small and cessation rates were higher in low SES group, then cessation rates not change in low SES group but increased in high SES group, leading to an increase in inequalities.

6.1.2.2 Geographic disparities

(Noonan et al. 2025)

Background

  • Providing updated estimates on differences in the use of cessation supports and quitting between urban and rural populations is a critical step in the tobacco control movement so that public health efforts can be shaped to better meet the unique cessation needs of rural populations

  • Little evidence on a national scale that has examined differences in cessation factors and quit ratios between rural and urban populations.

Aim

  • Examine the preva-lence of cessation supports, including both medicinal and behavioral, among those who currently smoke with a past year quit attempt(recently attempted to quit) by rural and urban status and geographic region

    => Odds of cessation are significantly higher among those who use cessation supports, so understanding differences in cessation support among rural/urban status can provide information for tailoring interventions to increase the use of cessation aids.

  • Examine lifetime quitting in rural versus urban peo-ple who smoke and by geographic region

Data

  • Nationally representative longitudinal cohort study that surveys tobacco use patterns and their associated effects on health outcomes of US youth and adults aged 12 and older

  • Adult respondents who reported current daily smoking (i.e., reported ever smoking a cigarette, having smoked more than 100 cigarettes in their lifetime,and currently smoking every day or someday smoking)

  • Reported a quit attempt in the past 12 months (recently attempted quitting)

  • Life-time quitting estimates were operationalized by the number of people who reported former smoking (defined as smoking 100 cigarettes in their lifetime and not currently smoking) divided by the number of people who ever smoked (defined as smoking at least 100 cigarettes in their lifetime).

Statistical method

  • cessation-related factors => chi-square test for association

  • Cessation-related factors between rural and urban were examined overall and within each geographic region

  • Quit ratios were also examined via unadjusted analyses and via multivariable logistic regression including the potential confounders such as age, gender, race/ethnicity, and educational attainment.

Results

  • Rural vs urban

    • people who smoke with a past year quit attempt were more likely to be White non-Hispanic race/ethnicity, have lower educational attainment, have greater cigarette dependence (based on average Cigarettes per Day(CPD) and time to first cigarette)

    • people who smoke with a past year quit attempt did not differ in terms of prevalence estimates of cessation supports (exception of a household smoking ban)

    • use of cessation medication (NRT, prescriptions) were 23% lower

    • using an e-cigarette to help quit smoking were 30% lower

    • rural versus urban succeeders greater adjusted odds of using medications (p = 0.0396) for quitting smoking.

Findings

  • lower adjusted odds of medication use to aid in quitting in rural versus urban people who smoke

  • when rural and urban participants are made more “similar” in terms of multiple social determinants of health(e.g., race, education) and cigarette dependence. Rurality emerges as an independent risk factor for lower medication use, similiar in using an e-cigarette to support quitting.

=> intersectionality in rural health and thus an important consideration when designing and evaluating interventions.

6.1.3 Previous studies in Korea

What research has been conducted on these topics among Korean populations, and what were the key findings?

(Lee et al. 2016)

Background

  • Even though the overall smoking rates have decreased in South Korea, socioeconomic inequality in smoking remains high (Khang and Cho 2006; Khang et al. 2009)

Aim

  • Attempted to examine indicators of socioeconomic status (SES), particularly education and other factors which affect smoking cessation in Korean men

  • Using longitudinal data for 4 years, we attempted to investigate inequalities in smoking cessation success by SES

Data

  • Current smokers were defined as adults who reported having smoked at least 100 cigarettes in their lifetime and now smoking every day or some days at baseline

  • Four waves of the Korea Health Panel (2009, 2010, 2011, 2012) conducted by the National Health Insurance Service and the Korea Institute for Health and Social Affairs.

Methodology

  • Outcomes: Short-term success at smoking

  • Independent variables: socio-demographic (SES(education level, income level and employment status) and marital status) and clinical (chronic disease) factors

  • Covariates: age, health insurance, residential area, cancers, circulatory system diseases, body mass index (BMI), and drinking frequency

  • generalized linear mixed model using smoking cessation between 2010 and 2012 as the dependent variable among smokers at the baseline in 2009

Findings:

  • number of chronic diseases showed a significant positive association with smoking cessation

  • men who are overweight or obese (OR = 1.529 (1.237 - 1.890))

  • Men who drink alcohol 2 or more times a week were less likely to stop smoking than those who were not drinking

  • Stratified by having chronic diseases

    • Men without chronic diseases: older than 65 years, with high school or above, overweight or obese (BMI ≥ 23) were more likely to quit

    • Men with chronic diseases: higher for those who were married or widowed/divorced/separated, overweight or obese

NoteSummary

In Korea men, factors related to smoking cessation (chronic diseases, BMI, year, education, marital status)

(Park et al. 2025)

Aim

  • Some previous studies investigated the disparities based on SES indicator but have not accounted for occupation level, so they did

  • Examine disparities in the intention to quit smoking, an indicator of health behavior, based on the aforementioned SES indicators(education, income, and occupation level), using absolute and relative indices of inequality.

Data

  • KNHANES: A stratified, multistage, clustered probability sampling design, based on region size and demographic characteristics was used to collect the preliminary data

Methodology

  • Socioeconomics indicators: education, income, occupation

  • Measures of inequalities: the slope index of inequality (SII), relative index of Inequality (RII), and odds ratio (OR)

  • the SII represents the prevalence difference, while the RII represents the prevalence ratio of health status between the lowest-ranking and highest-ranking groups

  • Socioeconomic inequalities (for education, income, and occupation) in intention to quit smoking within 6 months were measured using the following indices: adjusted odds ratio (aOR) between both groups using multivariable logistic regression, SII, RII

Findings:

  • observed significant socioeconomic inequality with the intention to quit smoking within 6 months

  • Income-related inequalities were not statistically significant, whereas education-related and occupation-related inequalities were statistically significant

  • There were inequalities in the intention to quit smoking plan depending on socioeconomic status, including education, household income, and occupational levels.

6.1.4 Definitions of smoking cessation outcomes

How have previous studies defined and measured successful and unsuccessful smoking cessation?

6.1.4.1 Smoking cessation

(Lee et al. 2016)

Short-term success at smoking cessation was assessed by surveying respondents who had not smoked for more than 6 months and were still not smoking

Smoking status was measured by asking respondents “Have you smoked at least 100 cigarettes in your life time?” and if respondents answered “yes”, they were asked “Do you now smoke every day, some days, or not at all?”

Those who reported having smoked at least 100 cigarettes in your lifetime and smoking at the time of the baseline survey in 2009 but not smoking at the time of the follow up survey were classified as former smokers

Smokers who reported smoking cigarettes every day or some days and former smokers who reported not smoking at the time of the survey but having not smoked for less than 6 months were considered current smokers.

(Jhun and Seo 2006) revealed that a relatively small number of Korean smokers were prepared to quit and the stage distribution might be influenced by demographic, socioeconomic, and smoking characteristics.

6.1.4.2 Quit intention

(Reid et al. 2010)

Quit intentions were derived from the survey item “Are you planning to quit smoking … within the next month, within the next 6 months, sometime in the future beyond 6 months, or are you not planning to quit?”. A dichotomous variable for any intentions (first three response categories) compared with no intentions (last category) was created

(Park et al. 2025)

intention to quit smoking within the next 6 months

“Do you plan to quit smoking within 6 months?”

The possible responses were:

  1. “I plan to quit smoking within 1 month”

  2. “I plan to quit smoking within 6 months”

  3. “I plan to quit smoking someday, but not within the next 6 months”;

  4. “I have no intention to quit smoking at the moment.”

We categorized responses as “Have plan” only for those who reported plans to quit smoking within either 1 month or 6 months.

6.2 Research question

Previous studies have identified various factors associated with successful smoking cessation, including demographic characteristics, physical and mental health conditions, nicotine dependence, alcohol consumption, attitudes and beliefs, and access to cessation support, including pharmacological and policy-based interventions (Vangeli et al. 2011; Hock et al. 2023; Kundu et al. 2024). However, smoking cessation outcomes are not equally distributed across socioeconomic and geographic groups. Socioeconomic disparities have been observed across educational and occupational groups. In the United States, individuals who experienced persistently low socioeconomic status (SES) throughout their life course were less likely to quit smoking than those with consistently high SES (Avila et al. 2022). Similar findings have been reported across European countries, where higher educational attainment and occupational status were positively associated with smoking cessation, as measured by the quit ratio (Bosdriesz et al. 2015). Regarding geographic disparities, previous research has reported lower utilization of certain smoking cessation support options among rural residents compared with their urban counterparts (Noonan et al. 2025).

In South Korea, previous studies have also examined factors associated with smoking cessation, including demographic characteristics, socioeconomic status, and alcohol consumption (Lee et al. 2016). Although smoking prevalence has declined, socioeconomic inequalities in smoking have persisted (Khang and Cho 2006; Khang et al. 2009). More recently, Park et al. (2025) reported significant socioeconomic inequalities in the intention to quit smoking within six months among Korean adults, particularly in relation to educational attainment and occupation (Park et al. 2025). From a geographic perspective, Kong and Cho (2021) found that smoking quit attempts in Korea were associated with community-level characteristics, including tobacco outlet density, as well as individual-level factors (Kong and Cho 2021).

6.3 Potential analytical approaches

Based on what type of data we have

Cross-sectional data:

  • The outcome would represent smoking cessation status at the time of the survey

  • applying a multilevel framework to smoking cessation status and by incorporating individual socioeconomic status, preventive behaviors, and geographic context (district-level of policy implementation followed WHO recommendation)

  • reference paper:

(Kim et al. 2018)

Derive contextual indicators of medical provider quality and assess their relative importance along with the individual utilization of antenatal care (ANC) and institutional births with a skilled birth attendant (SBA) in India using a multilevel framework

Outcome variables:

  • neonatal mortality (i.e., deaths within the first month)

  • infant mortality (i.e., deaths within one year since birth)

  • under-five child mortality (i.e., deaths within the first five years)

Individual access to care:

  • binary variable indicating whether the mother had <4 or≥ 4 ANC visits

  • binary variable was coded indicating whether the mother had birth delivery in an institutional setting with a SBA (doctor, nurse, or midwife).

Contextual provider quality

  • Indicators for outcome-specific provider quality (low versus high) were derived for each district and state by regression model with both predictor and outcome variables aggregated at higher levels (districts and states). Positive residuals = lower provider quality, negative residuals = high provider quality.

Methodology

  • model 1: null model

  • model 2: individual covariates (sex, birth order, birth interval, delivery method, maternal age, marital status, maternal education, maternal age at marriage, tetanus injection, household wealth)

  • model 3: individual covariates and ANC and SBA

  • model 4:

    • model 4A: model 3 + the fixed effect of provider quality indicators derived from ANC for districts

    • model 4B: model 3 + the fixed effect of provider quality indicators derived from ANC for states

  • model 5

    • model 5A: model 3 + the fixed effect of provider quality indicators derived from SBA for districts

    • model 5B: model 3 + the fixed effect of provider quality indicators derived from SBA for states

Results

  • most variation in neonatal mortality attributed to the community level (Variance Partitioning Coefficient [VPC]: 40.3%)

  • several individual-level covariates were significantly associated with increased odds of neonatal mortality

  • Failure to meet the WHO recommended 4 ANC visits was associated with 12% increased odds of neonatal mortality

  • Absence of SBA at delivery and births in non-institutional settings was associated with 4% increased odds of neonatal mortality

  • districts and states with low provider quality based on ANC were associated with almost 70% and 50% increased odds of neonatal mortality

  • districts and states with low provider quality based on SBA were associated with almost 80% and 60% increased odds of neonatal mortality

Longitudinal data:

  • We may conduct causal mediation analysis using g-computation in longitudinal settings with time-varying exposure, mediators, and confounders on the success quitting smoking.
