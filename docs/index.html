1.1 Literature review

(Choi, Oh, et al. 2025)

  • The Korean Code Against Cancer comprises a set of recommendations intended to prevent cancer.

  • The National Cancer Control Committee approved the currently published 10 codes for cancer prevention.

    1. no smoking and avoidance of secondhand smoke;
    2. consuming sufficient fruits and vegetables as part of a balanced diet;
    3. reducing salt intake and avoiding burnt or charred foods;
    4. limiting alcohol consumption;
    5. engaging in regular physical activity (at least 30 minutes a day, 5 days a week);
    6. maintaining a healthy body weight;
    7. receiving immunization against hepatitis B virus and human papillomavirus;
    8. practicing safe sex by maintaining a single sexual partner and using condoms;
    9. following health and safety guidelines to avoid exposure to occupational carcinogens;
    10. undergoing regular cancer screening

(Kwon et al. 2025)

  • mediation analysis: knowledge(exposure), attidude (mediator), practice (outcome) between 2014 and 2023 to assess both the direct and indirect effects of these variables on smoking behavior

  • only consider in ex-smoker and current-smoker

Finding:

  • Knowledge that smoking causes cancer was associated with a higher likelihood of having a positive attitude toward quitting smoking to prevent cancer

  • Knowledge was associated with a higher likelihood of practicing smoking cessation

  • Attitude was also associated with a higher likelihood of practicing smoking cessation

  • KAP model: attitude toward smoking cessation for cancer prevention mediated the relationship between knowledge of smoking’s role in causing cancer and the practice of smoking cessation

  • Prioritizing the identification of factors that influence smoking cessation behaviors is essential for the development and implementation of effective policies, as these factors may serve as mediating variables in smoking cessation practices

=> health education programs could effectively promote better attitudes and support healthy behaviors

  • Based on these findings, if current tobacco control policies have predominantly focused on “cigarettes” and reducing tobacco consumption among smokers, future policies should pivot toward “comprehensive regulation” and smoking prevention, emphasizing the importance of “attitudes” and “knowledge” among both smokers and non-smokers

(Hosseinpoor et al. 2012)

Background

  • Monitoring inequalities in non communicable disease risk factor prevalence can help to inform and target effective interventions. The prevalence of current daily smoking, low fruit and vegetable consumption, physical inactivity, and heavy episodic alcohol drinking were quantified and compared across wealth and education levels in low- and middle-income country groups

Methodology

  • Data were divided into four pooled datasets according to sex and LIC or MIC status, four NCD risk factors:

    • Current daily smoking was the current daily use of any tobacco product including cigarettes, cigars, pipes, and local tobacco products
    • Low fruit and vegetable consumption referred to an intake of fewer than five total servings of fruits and/or vegetables (about 400 grams) per day
    • Physical inactivity was classified as the failure to meet WHO recommendations on physical activity for health, which are defined as engaging in at least 150 minutes of moderate-intensity activity per week or 75 minutes of vigorous-intensity activity per week
    • Heavy episodic alcohol drinking was the consumption of five or more (in men) or four or more (in women) standard alcoholic drinks on at least one day during the preceding week

  • Socioeconomic status: household wealth status

  • Education was ranked according to five categories: no formal schooling, less than primary school, primary school completed, secondary/high school completed, and college completed or above.

  • Socioeconomic inequality in each risk factor prevalence was measured using the slope index of inequality (SII) and the relative index of inequality (RII)

  • SII and RII can be interpreted as the prevalence rate difference and the prevalence rate ratio

    • SII > 0 and RII > 1: regular inequality (i.e. an inverse association between socioeconomic status and risk factor prevalence)

    • Conversely, reverse inequality referred to higher risk factor prevalence among populations of higher socioeconomic position

Finding:

  • The strongest regular relative inequalities were reported for current daily smoking, particularly for educationrelated inequality.

  • Low fruit and vegetable consumption demonstrated regular inequality, with consistently higher prevalence among the disadvantaged across all study groups

  • For heavy episodic alcohol drinking, data indicated a mixed pattern of inequality

=> wealth- and education-related inequalities showed similar directional trends for each risk factor, with varying magnitude across sex-country income groups, indicating a concerted need for educational and povertyreduction oriented policies

1.3 Smoking-related knowledge, attitude, and practice for cancer prevention

  • Knowlegde:

Evaluated by assessing whether individuals were aware of the no-smoking guidelines and whether they understood that smoking causes cancer

  • Awareness:

The following questions were posed:

“Do you think smoking causes cancer?”

“Do you think that not smoking (i.e., avoiding smoking and secondhand smoke) prevents cancer?”

=> Knowledge of the link between smoking and cancer was determined by considering only those respondents who answered “yes” to these questions

  • Attitude:

Assessed by asking “Do you think about the recommendation to quit smoking for cancer prevention?” with the following response categories: “I do not think about it,” “I have not decided to quit smoking yet,” “I do not try to quit smoking,” and “I will quit smoking.”

=> positive attitude toward smoking cessation was operationally defined as responding “I will quit smoking.”

  • Practice:

Assessed by asking, “Have you ever smoked?” with the following response categories: “non-smoker,” “ex-smoker,” and “current smoker.”

This study included only individuals with a history of smoking, thereby excluding those who had never smoked.
